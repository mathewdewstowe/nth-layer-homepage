About this benchmark

The Nth Layer AI Maturity Benchmark measures how effectively PE-backed businesses are building, deploying, and scaling AI. Scores are based on 30 diagnostic questions across six pillars, benchmarked against 54 PE-backed SaaS businesses assessed over the past 18 months. Every score is derived from management responses and publicly observable evidence — not self-reported maturity claims.

Disclaimer: Cohort company benchmarks are derived from publicly available sources including company websites, investor materials, and published documentation. Data reflects information observable at the time of assessment and may be incomplete, outdated, or subject to interpretation. It should not be relied upon as a definitive or current statement of any company's position.

5 prioritised opportunities

01
Centralise your data estate
Critical

Your largest absolute gap and the single highest-leverage opportunity. Data infrastructure is the rate-limiter for every other pillar — without centralised, governed data, use cases cannot compound and models cannot improve. This gap alone explains most of the distance from the cohort mean.

First move

Audit your top 3 data domains, assign a data owner, and centralise with lineage documentation within 60 days. Kill any AI tooling procurement until this is complete.

In the cohort

Genesys (4.90), SailPoint (4.80), and Proofpoint (4.70) all score 5/5 — each has built a publicly documented centralised data layer as the explicit foundation for all AI initiatives.

02
Define your AI operating model
High

The single most common reason L3 businesses do not reach L4. Without a formal operating model — hub/spoke vs federated, named AI ownership, cross-functional squads — every new use case starts from scratch and CoE teams become bottlenecks rather than multipliers. The EBITDA is in the workflow redesign, not in the tools.

First move

Choose hub-and-spoke or federated model, assign a named AI owner with a budget line, and run one end-to-end workflow redesign sprint within 45 days.

In the cohort

Genesys (4.90) operates a federated model with named BU owners and a published ethics framework; IFS (4.20) and Visma (4.10) both run publicly documented hub-and-spoke AI structures.

03
Formalise P&L attribution for every AI initiative
Medium

You are at cohort level on use cases but the gap to L4 in this pillar is not volume — it is measurement. Most L3 businesses have AI in production but cannot attribute EBITDA to it. Without formal P&L lines, use cases cannot be prioritised, scaled, or defended at board level, and the programme stalls at L3 regardless of how many initiatives are running.

First move

Assign a finance owner to each production use case. Establish a P&L line per initiative within 30 days. Report at every board cycle.

In the cohort

Zendesk (4.25) attributes AI resolution rates to revenue in investor filings; Coupa (4.15) publishes per-customer AI savings data — creating a direct P&L link that has become a core sales asset.

04
Build a lightweight governance framework
Low

You are below cohort but close. Governance is a hard gate for L4 classification — without it, the next deployment incident becomes a board conversation rather than a managed process. Businesses without a governance framework are also increasingly exposed to customer due diligence and regulatory scrutiny during exit processes.

First move

Classify all existing AI deployments by risk tier, draft an acceptable-use policy, and assign a named risk owner within 60 days.

In the cohort

SailPoint (4.80) publishes AI governance principles and maintains an external ethics board; The Access Group (4.20) has documented acceptable-use frameworks across HR, healthcare, and legal products.

05
Formalise AI strategy with board-level KPIs
Low

Strategy is broadly at cohort level but the transition to L4 requires more than informal ambition — a formal strategy document, exec accountability, and board-level KPIs. L3 strategies often dissipate when sponsors change; formalisation protects the programme.

First move

Publish a one-page AI strategy with a named exec sponsor, three-year targets, and quarterly board reporting. Due at next board cycle.

In the cohort

IFS (4.20) publishes their IFS.ai strategy with a named exec owner and public roadmap; Visma (4.10) runs a group-wide AI strategy with per-company accountability across 170+ portfolio companies.

90-day action plan

01
Design your AI operating model. 30 DAYS

Hub-and-spoke or federated? Define who owns AI capability vs who deploys it. Most L3 companies have a CoE that is a bottleneck, not an enabler. Assign a named owner with a budget line and run one end-to-end workflow redesign sprint. The org structure must match the ambition.

02
Formalise P&L attribution for every AI initiative. 60 DAYS

If you cannot measure it, you cannot manage it and you cannot defend it to the board. Finance needs to own the metric. Assign a P&L line to each production use case and establish a reporting cadence. This is the single most common gap between L3 and L4 in the cohort.

03
Launch your governance framework. 90 DAYS

Risk classification, acceptable use, model validation sign-off. Without governance the next deployment incident becomes a hold-period story. With it, you have a defensible programme and a credible foundation for L4 classification.

What L4 looks like

At L4 — Systemic — AI is a measurable EBITDA contributor with formal P&L attribution, a defined operating model, and a governance framework that survives management changes. The trap at L3 is treating AI as an IT project rather than an operating-model transformation. The 9 companies in the cohort that have reached L4 average 4.26 overall, versus 3.39 for the cohort. Their weakest pillar on average is Talent & Operating Model (3.89) — which is also the most common gap for L3 companies moving up. The structural difference between L3 and L4 is not ambition or tooling — it is operating model formalisation and measurable P&L attribution.
