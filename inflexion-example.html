About this benchmark

The Nth Layer AI Maturity Benchmark measures how effectively PE-backed businesses are building, deploying, and scaling AI. Scores are based on 30 diagnostic questions across six pillars, benchmarked against 54 PE-backed SaaS businesses assessed over the past 18 months. Every score is derived from management responses and publicly observable evidence, not self-reported maturity claims.

Disclaimer: Cohort company benchmarks are derived from publicly available sources including company websites, investor materials, and published documentation. Data reflects information observable at the time of assessment and may be incomplete, outdated, or subject to interpretation. It should not be relied upon as a definitive or current statement of any company's position.

Peer analysis

You are at the 12th percentile of the 54-company cohort, and below cohort average in all six pillars. Largest gaps in Data & Knowledge Foundation (−1.55) and Strategy & Leadership (−1.13). Those two pillars carry a combined weight that limits every other area; without centralised data and a formal strategy, use cases cannot compound and talent is uncoordinated. Use Cases & Value Capture is the relative strength at 3.0/5.0, suggesting pilots exist and are generating signal; the challenge is converting that signal into a scalable, production-grade programme. The 22 other companies in this cohort at L2 are predominantly in the same position: activity without infrastructure.

5 prioritised opportunities

01
Build a centralised data foundation
Critical

Your largest absolute gap at −1.55 vs cohort. Data infrastructure is the rate-limiter for every other pillar; without it, pilots cannot connect, models cannot improve, and use cases cannot compound. At 1.8/5.0 you have the data but it is not structured, owned, or accessible enough to support production-grade AI. This is the precondition for the L2→L3 transition.

First move

Map your top 5 data domains. Assign a named data owner to each. Centralise your highest-value domain with lineage documentation within 60 days. No new AI tool procurement until this is done.

In the cohort

Genesys (4.90), SailPoint (4.80), and Proofpoint (4.70) all score 5/5; each has built a publicly documented centralised data layer as the explicit foundation for all AI initiatives: Genesys via a unified cloud platform, SailPoint via their Atlas identity data lake, Proofpoint via their threat intelligence data fabric.

02
Appoint an AI owner and formalise strategy
High

At −1.13 vs cohort, Strategy & Leadership is your second-largest gap. Most L2 businesses run AI as a distributed experiment with no single owner, which means no prioritisation, no budget discipline, and no board accountability. A named owner with a strategy document and three-year targets is the structural precondition for making the L3 transition stick.

First move

Appoint a named AI lead; this does not require a new hire. Draft a one-page AI strategy with exec sponsorship, three annual targets, and a reporting cadence at every board cycle.

In the cohort

IFS (4.20) publishes their IFS.ai strategy with a named executive owner, a public AI roadmap, and board-level reporting. Visma (4.10) runs a group-wide AI strategy with individual company accountability across 170+ portfolio companies, a model that has enabled consistent adoption across a decentralised structure.

03
Define your AI operating model
High

Talent & Operating Model sits at 2.0/5.0, below cohort by 0.89 points. Without a formal operating model, every new initiative starts from scratch: no shared tooling, no reusable components, no cross-functional accountability. At L2, the typical failure mode is a capable technical team with no mechanism for translating their work into business impact. The operating model is the mechanism.

First move

Choose hub-and-spoke or federated. Assign one named business owner per active AI initiative. Run a single end-to-end workflow redesign sprint within 45 days; the goal is proof of model, not breadth of coverage.

In the cohort

Genesys (4.90) operates a federated model with named BU owners and a published ethics framework. IFS (4.20) and Visma (4.10) both run publicly documented hub-and-spoke AI operating structures with defined handoffs between central capability and business-unit delivery.

04
Establish a lightweight governance baseline
Medium

Governance at 2.5/5.0 is below cohort by 0.75 points. For an HR SaaS business, this is an accelerating risk; customers, regulators, and prospective buyers are all increasing scrutiny of AI governance in workforce-related tooling. An incident without a framework in place becomes a board conversation rather than a managed process. The barrier to a basic governance framework is low; the cost of not having one is rising.

First move

Classify every active AI deployment by risk tier. Draft an acceptable-use policy specific to your HR and workforce context. Assign a named risk owner. Target 60 days.

In the cohort

SailPoint (4.80) publishes AI governance principles and maintains an external ethics advisory board. The Access Group (4.20) has documented acceptable-use frameworks across HR, healthcare, and legal products, a directly relevant model for Meridian's sector profile.

05
Strengthen integration infrastructure
Low

Infrastructure & Tooling at 2.8/5.0 is your strongest relative position at −0.70 vs cohort, but still a constraint. The gap is less about tools and more about integration: AI deployments at L2 are typically isolated, not connected to core systems in a way that allows automation, feedback loops, or scale. Strengthening integration infrastructure now means the use cases you build in opportunities 1–3 can actually compound.

First move

Audit the integration layer between your top AI deployment and your core product. Identify the two connection points that, if automated, would create a data feedback loop. Scope and build within 90 days.

In the cohort

Genesys (4.90), SailPoint (4.80), and The Access Group (4.20) all score 5/5 on infrastructure; each has invested in integration layers that allow AI tooling to connect across their product and data stack rather than operating as isolated deployments.

90-day action plan

01
Appoint an AI lead and complete a data estate audit. 30 DAYS

The single most common reason L2 pilots stall is that no one owns them. Appoint a named AI lead with a budget line; this does not need to be a new hire. Simultaneously, complete a data estate audit: map your top five data domains, identify ownership gaps, and score each for AI readiness. This is the precondition for everything else in this plan. Without it, every subsequent step will be constrained by data access problems that were not anticipated.

02
Convert your best pilot to a production-grade deployment. 60 DAYS

Identify the single AI initiative generating the clearest signal and convert it: assign a finance owner, define success metrics, establish a reporting cadence, and connect it to your product integration layer. In parallel, draft a one-page AI strategy with exec sponsorship and three annual targets. Both outputs should exist before the next board cycle. The goal is not breadth; it is one demonstrably production-grade deployment with measurable output and a named owner.

03
Build your data foundation and governance baseline. 90 DAYS

Centralise your highest-value data domain with lineage documentation. Draft an acceptable-use policy for your HR and workforce context, classify existing AI deployments by risk tier, and assign a named risk owner. With these three outputs in place (a named AI owner, a production deployment, a governed data domain) you have the structural foundations for L3 classification: AI in production with a measurable, governed operating model behind it.

What L3 looks like

At L3 (Operational), AI is in production with real user adoption, and the business has measurable signal about what is working. The transition from L2 to L3 is not about adding more pilots; it is about converting the best ones into production-grade, measurable workflows with a defined operating model to sustain them. 61% of this cohort sits at L3. The most common path is consistent across nearly every business that has made the transition: one named owner, one centralised data domain, and one production use case with a finance-owned P&L line. Those three steps, executed in sequence, define the L2→L3 transition for the majority of businesses in this cohort.
